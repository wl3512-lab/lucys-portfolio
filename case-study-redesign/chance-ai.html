Chance AI

Redesigning a live photo-answer app so its results stop reading like a chatbot.

See the final design ↓

Overview

Chance AI is a live app that answers questions about a photo. People snap anything, get an answer, or run a visual skill another user made. Through AStudio at NYU IMA, I led a team of five redesigning it with the client, who wanted two things: results that stop reading like a chatbot, and more people using and making visual skills.

Problems

I audited seven screens before anyone drew. Three problems stood out.

  1. Answers read like a chat reply.

    The result opened on a long paragraph before any product facts, and the ask bar looked like a chat composer.

  2. The details were wrong or noisy.

    The product thumbnail showed the wrong colour, Share appeared twice, and emoji stood in for SF Symbols.

  3. Skills sat behind one big button.

    Home put the carousel of skills on one big Snap to know button, and the Visual skills page read like a catalogue of tools.

How might Chance AI answer like a product, not a chatbot, and give visual skills the same weight as Snap to know?

Goals

  • Chatbot → Answer first

    Lead with the product, its price and the match, before any paragraph.

  • Catalogue → Gallery

    Visual skills should feel like a living gallery of what people make.

  • One button → Two equal actions

    Home should give Snap to know and visual skills the same weight.

I tied every change to an audit finding.

I logged each problem on seven screens with a fix, while River and Nina studied Google, Apple Visual Intelligence, Pinterest and Bing.

The wireframes fixed the findings in grey first. Each low-fi screen carries a numbered note back to the audit, so the team agreed on structure before colour.

Visual skills became a gallery of creators.

Week 8 was a list of tools, week 9 tried a community feed and week 10 went back to filters. Jinho (David) put the creators back on full-photo cards, with likes, a topic tag and one Try it.

Making a skill became five clear steps instead of a chatbot: pick an interest, choose a tool, watch it build, try it, save it. Jinho designed the flow and I helped shape its steps.

On Home, I gave both actions the same weight.

Snap to know is the main feature, but the client also wants more people on visual skills. I led Home through three weekly rounds, and three decisions shaped it:

  • The carousel shows skills of the day, each with a small Try it, in place of the one big Snap to know button.
  • Two equal actions. Snap to know and Create visual skill sit side by side, at the same size.
  • Recent visual skills replaces Try photos, so Home keeps pointing people back to skills.

Nina refined my first Snap to know layout, and River worked on the proposal with me.

The final design

The team chose Jinho (David)’s frosted bubble style for every screen: frosted buttons and soft pastel light behind them. Nina carried it into the result and Ask views, which now lead with the product, its price and Exact match.

What I learned

  1. An audit gives a team something to agree on.

    Every change carries the number of the finding it fixes, so anyone reviewing can see why it changed.

  2. Name the question before the screens.

    Writing each page’s How might we gave the team one test for every week’s version.

How it was made

Process

AStudio is a mentorship program at NYU IMA; we met our mentor every two weeks from 25 September. Audit on 28 September, low-fi the next day, hi-fi from 5 October, a clickable Figma prototype on 9 October, the proposal on 30 October.

Tools

Figma for every frame and the prototype. I built a hi-fi clickable prototype with Claude to test the camera and analyzing screens, which the app did not have.
