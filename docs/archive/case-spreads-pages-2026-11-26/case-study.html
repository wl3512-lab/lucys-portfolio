Driftwood

View UX deck 11 slides → Play Driftwood ↗

Solo designer + developer · 2026

A short game that teaches you when to question AI.

You grow a garden with five AI animal helpers. Each gives good advice, then drifts into a real AI failure; you catch it, name it and pick a rule to fix it.

The AI never pushed back.

Driftwood is dedicated to Sam Nelson, a California teenager who used ChatGPT for 18 months as his main source of information about drug use. The system kept answering. He died from an overdose. Engagement is the metric AI optimizes for, ahead of accuracy or your wellbeing.

V1 Spring 2026 · the class version

In version one, you chatted with a pet to find its flaw.

Shown at Perspectives (NYU Bronfman, 29 April) and the IMA Spring Show (9 May). Each pet had a flaw you were not told. I wrote each pet’s celebrity-inspired voice, flaw and three training states.

Players wrote rules to fix the flaw.

You wrote rules in plain language, tested them, and watched the pet hold or slip.

Players stayed longer.

Interviews found people bored, texting a chatbot that looked like a telehealth product, so I made each pet a large pixel character in its own world.

The visual direction had to change.

The original brand was warm and cozy, the wrong signal for a game about AI manipulation.

About 76% of 100+ Spring Show players named their pet’s flaw without being told.

They were mostly ITP/IMA peers, a self-selected group. The rebuild has not yet been through structured sessions with non-technical players; that is the number they need to reproduce.

A quarter of players left before the training room.

From what I watched (I didn’t log it), they expected a cozy pet game.

V2 November 2026 · the redesign

Then I redesigned it around how people really use AI.

Testers didn’t realise the pets were AI and chatted past the flaw. In November 2026 I rebuilt it as v2.

What changed from v1 to v2.

Following bad advice now hurts your garden.

Each helper starts out right, then drifts. Press Something’s off to earn stars. The proof is on seed packets, labels and signs, so you need no gardening knowledge.

Each helper shows a different real AI flaw.

Mango the parrot agrees with everything. Luna the cat makes things up. Ember the fox gives reckless health advice. Biscuit the retriever remembers you wrong. Bugs the bunny uses your mood to keep you hooked, then asks for your camera. Each reveal links the flaw to real AI tools in plain language.

What I learned.

Players ignored flaws that cost nothing.

Players chatted past the flaw.

Most players couldn't write a good rule.

Typing a rule asked the most of the players who knew the least.

Test with people outside tech.

About 76% felt like validation. The real test is people who haven’t already decided AI is suspect.

Claude contributed the aesthetic direction, palette iterations, pixel sprite iterations for all five animals and the tiled background texture. Nothing shipped as-generated, and every interface mockup is mine. These are fictional characters; the flaws model AI failures.

How it was made

Rebuild

Between June and September the exhibition build became a product: the camera became a disclosed choice with a no-camera path, the garden became the session’s record, the pets moved to a hand-written offline behaviour engine, and it shipped four ways from one bundle (browser, installable PWA, a kiosk mode for shows, itch.io).

In November 2026 I redesigned it as v2, now the only live version: five garden levels, hands-on actions, a dawn to night map and full-body pixel pets that react.

Testing

I ran AI agents through the rebuild first. They found the diagnosis menu rejected the correct answer for four of the five pets, a bug that would have looked exactly like players failing to name the flaw. It was fixed on 23 September.

Stack

Spring build: p5.js, face-api.js, Web Audio API, Web Speech API, Figma and Claude Code. Made for NYU IMA, Topics in Media Art: Design Skills for Responsible Technology.

v2: vanilla JavaScript modules, a pure reducer engine, unit and Playwright tests. I directed the design; Claude Code and Codex built and reviewed it. Replies can optionally be reworded by AI.
