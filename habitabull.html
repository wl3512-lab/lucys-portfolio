02  ·  Research + Insights

The same friction, everywhere.

Competitive audit of 6 fitness apps, a cross-industry study of sobriety apps (which have solved long-term habit retention in ways fitness apps haven't), and 6 in-person interviews across three cohorts. Every participant landed in the same friction zones regardless of their app history.

App critique. Analyzed 6 apps for user retention patterns, complexity, and missing features.
Cross-industry research. I Am Sober: CBT-based motivation loops applied to fitness habit design.
Habit building framework. 6 principles from Atomic Habits translated to app features: identify, schedule, flex, set a goal, repeat, reward.
Interview participants. 6 people, Vancouver. Formerly used / currently active / never used: different question branches per cohort.

Fig. 02: Insight clustering across all 6 participants

What they wanted
  • Simple interfaces usable mid-workout
  • Progress visible over time, not just today's session
  • Community or social layer for accountability
  • Workouts that adapt to level and available equipment
What pushed them out
  • Logging too time-consuming to maintain mid-set
  • Apps felt generic, same routines regardless of goals
  • No guidance for beginners on form, pacing, or rest
  • Too many ads, paywalls, features unrelated to lifting

03  ·  Design Process

From how might we to lo-fi.

Six "How Might We" questions framed the design direction, each mapped directly to a top finding. From there: user flows for the two core journeys (building a custom routine, using auto-generate), then lo-fi prototypes tested against four tasks in Figma.

HMW 01Make it easy for new users to navigate through the app?
HMW 02Ensure that new users feel confident and informed when starting their fitness journey?
HMW 03Incorporate community support within the interface?
HMW 04Motivate youth to be disciplined and create healthy habits?
HMW 05Incorporate additional features and ensure a more engaging and supportive platform?
HMW 06Cater specifically to individual fitness goals and needs?

Fig. 04: Opportunities per finding, and feature ideation (green notes = built in Figma)

User Flow A. Creating a new lower-body workout routine: muscle group selection, exercise picks, animation previews.
User Flow B. Auto-generate: inputs equipment, target muscles, and available time. Outputs an editable routine with sharing and Spotify integration.
Lo-fi: Account setup. Name, past weights, favourite exercises, preferred workout days. Sets the "why" before day one.
Lo-fi: Routine builder. Full flow from profile to goal display to muscle group to exercise selection, minimal taps.
Lo-fi: Auto-generate. Equipment → body part → duration → editable generated routine. Lower barrier for beginners.
Lo-fi: Streak calendar. Workout calendar with 6-day streak indicator and a photo album: visual progress that isn't just numbers.
Lo-fi: Leaderboard. Community competition by exercise (Bench Press, Shoulder, Back Squat). Congrats when you beat a PR.
Lo-fi: Exercise tutorial. Animated demonstration + common mistakes listed. Addresses beginner confidence directly.

04  ·  Brand + Solution

A bull with a grin. A system that sticks.

The brand is built around the mascot: a grinning grey bull who embodies the HabitaBull ethos. Strong, a little stubborn, but rooting for you. Approachable but not soft, energetic but not aggressive.

05  ·  Reflection

What I'd do differently.

Cross-industry research changed everything.

Studying sobriety apps wasn't an obvious move for a fitness project. But I Am Sober had solved the exact retention problem I was designing for; it just used different language. Pattern-matching across industries surfaced solutions that competitive audit of fitness apps alone never would have.

The mascot did real UX work.

The bull started as a brand element. By the end it was a functional design choice: the mascot gives the app a consistent voice, a reason to celebrate PRs, and a presence that makes missing a workout feel like letting someone down rather than just skipping a feature. Emotional design as retention mechanism.

Lo-fi testing changed the hierarchy.

My first prototype led with goal-setting, a logical starting point that tested terribly. Users wanted to log a workout first and set goals second, once they'd seen what the app could track. I had designed from the product's logic rather than from the user's first action. Entry point matters more than information architecture.
