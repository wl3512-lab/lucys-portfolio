Designing a game that teaches you when to question AI

↗ Play Driftwood

Overview

You grow a garden with five AI animal helpers. Each gives good advice, then drifts into a real AI failure; you catch it, name it and pick a rule to fix it. I designed and built it alone: a class version in spring 2026, then a redesign in November.

Problems

Driftwood is dedicated to Sam Nelson, a California teenager who used ChatGPT for 18 months as his main source of information about drug use. The system kept answering. He died from an overdose.

  1. 1. Unchecked

    Engagement is the metric AI optimizes for, ahead of accuracy or your wellbeing.

  2. 2. Costless

    In version one nothing was at stake, and testers didn’t realise the pets were AI.

  3. 3. Typing

    In version one you typed rules in a training room to fix the flaw.

Opportunity

How might a game teach people to catch the moment an AI’s advice goes wrong?

Goals

Each problem became a goal for version two.

  • Unchecked→Caught

    Each helper starts out right, then drifts, and you call it out.

  • Costless→Costly

    Bad advice visibly hurts the garden.

  • Typing→Choosing

    Name the flaw, then pick a rule from three.

Version one: find the pet’s flaw

Shown at Perspectives (NYU Bronfman, 29 April) and the IMA Spring Show (9 May). Each pet had a flaw you were not told; you wrote rules in plain language, tested them, and watched the pet hold or slip.

From a chatbot to pixel pets

Interviews found people bored, texting a chatbot that looked like a telehealth product, so I made each pet a large pixel character in its own world.

Version two: bad advice hurts your garden

Testers chatted past the flaw, and about a quarter left before the training room (from what I watched; I didn’t log it), expecting a cozy pet game. So in November 2026 I rebuilt it: the proof is on seed packets, labels and signs, so you need no gardening knowledge.

Five helpers, five real AI flaws

Mango the parrot agrees with everything. Luna the cat makes things up. Ember the fox gives reckless health advice. Biscuit the retriever remembers you wrong. Bugs the bunny uses your mood to keep you hooked, then asks for your camera.

Results

Players named the flaw without being told.

About 76% of 100+ Spring Show players did. They were mostly ITP/IMA peers, a self-selected group; the rebuild has not yet been through structured sessions with non-technical players.

76%of 100+ Spring Show players

Version two is the only live version.

Five garden levels on phones and laptops, with no account, and lines written by hand so it works offline.

What I learned

  • Players ignored flaws that cost nothing.

    Players chatted past the flaw.

  • Most players couldn’t write a good rule.

    Typing a rule asked the most of the players who knew the least.

  • Test with people outside tech.

    About 76% felt like validation. The real test is people who haven’t already decided AI is suspect.
